A · Measuring prices

The Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices

Inflation and deflation describe changes in the level of final prices, measured by the HICP.

  • Any good representing 1/1000th of household consumption must be included.
  • Prices \(p_i\) are weighted by their share \(w_i\) in household spending:
\[ HICP = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\, p_i \]
  • In France, N = 1,700 goods and services.
  • Computed by national agencies (INSEE in France), consolidated at European level (Eurostat).

Inflation vs deflation

Year-on-year change of the index:

Inflation

\[ \frac{HICP_t - HICP_{t-12}}{HICP_{t-12}} \times 100 > 0 \]

Deflation

\[ \frac{HICP_t - HICP_{t-12}}{HICP_{t-12}} \times 100 < 0 \]

(monthly data, \(t-12\) = same month one year earlier)

B · Inflation and balance sheets

Nominal vs real: what +10% inflation did

\[ \text{Real value} = \frac{\text{Nominal value}}{1+\pi} \]

With \(\pi = 10\%\), the price level goes from 1 to 1.1: what cost $1 a year ago now costs $1.10. So $1 received at year end only buys \(1/1.1 = 0.91\) of last year’s basket. Dividing by 1.1 converts end-of-year dollars into start-of-year purchasing power.

Bank · loses

Its $5 profit is worth \(5/1.1 = 4.55\) — 91% of the purchasing power it commanded a year ago.

Firm · gains

Real profit \(16/1.1 = 14.5\), far above the $5 it would earn with zero inflation.

Households · lose

The same output now costs $121 instead of $110: their assets fall — or their debt rises — by $11.

B · The other side

Deflation breaks the monetary circuit

Deflation often comes with falling wages, revenues and profits. Back to our example, with prices down 10%:

  • Output sells for $99 instead of $110.
  • The firm owes $105 but has only $99 → it cannot repay: insolvent, liabilities exceed assets.
  • The bank created $100 of deposits but only $99 come back → it books a loss and must borrow on the interbank market (or use its provisions).

C · Firms

Firms: produce more — the Phillips curve

With higher profits and rising prices, firms want to increase production: more raw materials and labour → unemployment probably falls.

Inflation→Profits→Investment→Unemployment

This inverse relation between inflation and unemployment is the Phillips curve.

Figure: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis
C · Banks

Banks: protect the real rate — the Fisher effect

Inflation lowers the real interest rate. Banks react by raising the nominal rate to protect profits — as long as the central bank’s base rate does not rise.

Inflation→Real rate→Profits→Nominal rates→Profits
\[ 1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi) \;\Rightarrow\; i \approx r + \pi \]

\(i\) nominal rate · \(r\) real rate · \(\pi\) inflation. Fisher effect.

Try it: real cost of borrowing

Real rate \(r = \frac{1+i}{1+\pi}-1\)

C · Central bank

The central bank: a 2% mandate

The ECB’s mandate is to keep inflation at 2%. Its main instrument is the refinancing rate — the rate at which banks borrow reserves.

Inflation→Refinancing rate→Bank lending rates→Firm profits→Investment→Inflation

Higher lending rates raise the cost of credit for firms → production may fall → weaker demand for goods and labour → prices rise more slowly, or even fall.

C · Households

Households: the wage–price spiral

Households lose purchasing power → trade unions demand wage indexation → a spiral if firms raise prices further.

Inflation→Purchasing power→Renegotiation→Wages→Profits→Inflation ↺

But trade unions have limited bargaining power: wages usually do not fully adjust.

“Wages go up the stairs, but prices take the elevator.”
— Alfred Sauvy

D · Is it the money supply?

The quantity theory of money: \(MV = PT\)

\[ \underbrace{M}_{\text{money stock}} \times \underbrace{V}_{\text{velocity}} = \underbrace{P}_{\text{price level}} \times \underbrace{T}_{\text{transactions}} \]

Fisher’s equation of exchange is an identity: the money spent (\(MV\)) equals the value of what is bought (\(PT\)).

It becomes a theory of inflation with three assumptions:

  • \(V\) is stable (payment habits change slowly);
  • \(T\) is set by real factors (full employment);
  • causality runs from \(M\) to \(P\): the central bank controls the money stock.
Prediction
\[ \pi \approx g_M + g_V - g_T \;\approx\; g_M - g_T \]

Inflation = money growth in excess of real growth.

“Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.”
— Milton Friedman

→ Testable: money growth and inflation should move together. Do they?

D · A real-sector model

Prices = mark-up × unit costs

Firms set prices given their profit target (the mark-up) over unit labour and material costs:

\[ P \times Y = M \times (W + U) \;\Rightarrow\; P = M \times \frac{W+U}{Y} \]
Log-linearising and taking first differences: \[ \Delta p = \Delta w + \Delta u + \Delta m - \Delta y \]
  • \(P\) price level
  • \(Y\) real output (GDP)
  • \(M\) target mark-up
  • \(W\) wage bill
  • \(U\) cost of raw materials

Inflation (\(\Delta p>0\)) occurs whenever costs (\(w,u\)) and mark-ups (\(m\)) grow faster than output (\(y\)).

D · Channels

Understanding the causes of inflation

\[ \Delta p = \underbrace{\Delta w + \Delta u - \Delta y}_{\text{unit costs}} + \underbrace{\Delta m}_{\text{mark-up}} \]

Material costs \(u\)

A rise in the price of one raw material is only one component of \(u\): alone it doesn’t make inflation — but with constant wages and profit targets, the price level shifts up as costs pass through to customers.

Wages \(w\)

Wage increases raise inflation if firms roll the cost over to their prices.

The exchange rate

A depreciation raises import prices (\(u\)↑). Domestic firms facing less foreign competition may raise their own prices too (\(m\)↑) and pocket windfall profits.

Indexation

If consumers demand higher wages in return, a spiral may follow. Wage indexation was very likely behind the very high inflation of the 1970s.

D · Expectations

Inflation expectations: the adaptive hypothesis

Workers and firms set wages and prices given their expected inflation:

\[ \pi^e_t = \pi^e_{t-1} + x\,(\pi_{t-1} - \pi^e_{t-1}) \]

\(\pi^e\) expected inflation · \(\pi\) actual inflation · \(0 \le x \le 1\) speed of adjustment

Expectations lag behind actual inflation: agents correct only a share \(x\) of last year’s error. With \(x=1\), expected = last year’s inflation.

Actual: euro area HICP, annual average (Eurostat). Expected: simulated, starting at 2% in 2012.
