A · The method

Three rules of stock-flow consistent (SFC) modelling

1

Every flow has a counterpart

A payment is an outflow for one sector and an inflow for another. Nothing leaks out of the system.

2

Stocks cumulate flows

This period’s wealth is last period’s wealth plus this period’s saving: \(V = V_{-1} + \Delta V\).

3

Accounts first, behaviour second

Write down the matrices, then add behavioural assumptions to “close” them into a model.

Key idea

The accounting removes degrees of freedom

Once every flow is recorded twice, many outcomes that look possible on paper simply cannot happen. Class 4’s sectoral-balances identity was a first example; today we build a full model on the same principle (Godley & Lavoie; the term “SFC” is due to Dos Santos, 2002).

Link back to Class 4: private + public + external balances = 0. SFC models are that identity plus stocks, plus behaviour.
A · The model economy

One small open economy, five sectors

Simplifying assumptions (pp. 243–244):

  • Fixed exchange rate, no private capital flows: residents hold no foreign assets, foreigners hold no domestic debt
  • No commercial banks, no investment, no price changes
  • The central bank sets the interest rate \(r\)
  • Think of a developing economy before financial liberalisation

Reading a row
T-bills are a liability of the government (−B) and an asset for whoever holds them: households (+Bp) and the central bank (+Bcb). So \(B_p + B_{cb} - B = 0\).

Assets minus liabilities, sector by sector: the central bank’s net worth is zero, the government’s is −B and the rest of the world’s is −R. Since the row sums to zero, household wealth is \(V = B + R\).

C · The real side

Five equations determine income and wealth

(1)\(Y = C + G + X - IM\)GDP: domestic output equals demand for it
(2)\(\Delta V = Y - T - C\)Household saving adds to wealth
(3)\(T = \theta \cdot Y \qquad 0<\theta<1\)Taxes, with tax rate \(\theta\)
(4)\(IM = \mu \cdot Y \qquad 0<\mu<1\)Imports, with propensity to import \(\mu\)
(5)\(C = \alpha_1 (1-\theta) Y + \alpha_2 V_{-1}\)Consumption out of disposable income and out of last period’s wealth
Key idea

The lagged stock \(V_{-1}\) is what makes the model move through time

Today’s spending depends on yesterday’s wealth, and today’s saving changes tomorrow’s wealth. \(G\) and \(X\) are exogenous: decided by the government and by foreign demand.

Do not classify the equations into identities vs behavioural here — that is assignment question 3. If asked, hint: which of these could be false?
C · Solving the real side

The open-economy multiplier

Substitute (3), (4) and (5) into (1):

\[Y = m\,\big(G + X + \alpha_2 V_{-1}\big)\]
\[m = \frac{1}{1-\alpha_1(1-\theta)+\mu}\]

With \(\alpha_1 = 0.8,\ \theta = 0.2,\ \mu = 0.25\): \(m = 1/0.61 \approx 1.64\).

Short run

Given last period’s wealth, \(Y = 1.64 \times (20 + 25 + 0.16 \times 100) = 100\).

Steady state

Wealth stops changing when its ratio to disposable income reaches the target \(\alpha_3 = (1-\alpha_1)/\alpha_2 = 1.25\). Then

\(Y^* = \dfrac{G + X}{\theta + \mu} = \dfrac{45}{0.45} = 100\)

Leave the comparative statics of m (what happens when each parameter changes) to the students: assignment question 4. You can ask them which parameters appear in m but not in Y*.
C · The financial side

Where does saving go? Portfolio choice and the central bank

(10)\(B_{p,d} = V\,\big[(1-\lambda_0) + \lambda_1 r\big]\)Household demand for bills: falls with liquidity preference \(\lambda_0\), rises with the interest rate
(11)\(H_d = V - B_{p,d}\)Cash is the residual: one decision, not two
(12)\(\Delta B_s = G - T\)The government issues bills to cover its deficit
(13)\(B_{p,s} = B_{p,d}\)At the target rate, households get the bills they want
(14)\(B_{cb,s} = B_s - B_{p,s}\)The central bank takes the rest…
(15)\(B_{cb,d} = B_{cb,s}\)… whatever the amount
(16)\(\Delta R = X - IM\)No capital flows: trade imbalances change reserves
(17)\(H_s = R + B_{cb}\)Cash supply comes from the central bank’s balance sheet

\(\lambda_0\) is households’ liquidity preference: it shifts the demand for cash, so a higher \(\lambda_0\) means households hold a larger share of their wealth as cash at any given interest rate. \(\lambda_1\) measures how much the bill share rises with \(r\). Baseline: \(\lambda_0 = 0.4,\ \lambda_1 = 5,\ r = 2\%\), so households keep \(0.6 + 5 \times 0.02 = 70\%\) of their wealth in bills.

C · Closing the model

The hidden equation

Count: every endogenous variable now has its own equation. Yet one condition is still missing from the list:

\[H_s = H_d\]

We must not add it. It is implied by all the other equations, because the accounting is complete: if every other row and column balances, the last one must balance too.

In the spreadsheet it is never imposed. It is computed and checked: if \(H_d - H_s \neq 0\), a formula is wrong.

Key idea

Money is endogenous

The central bank fixes the price of money (\(r\)), not its quantity. The stock of cash is whatever households want to hold at that rate. The central bank supplies it by adjusting how many T-bills it holds.

Analogy with Walras’ law: with n markets, n−1 clearing conditions suffice. The paper calls it the redundant equation.
Check

The central bank raises \(r\) from 2% to 3%. Wealth stays at 100 and nothing else changes yet. What happens to the central bank’s holdings of T-bills?

C · Endogenous money, step by step

Why the money stock falls when \(r\) rises

1 · Households rebalance their portfolio. Cash pays nothing; bills now pay 3% instead of 2%. Holding cash has become more costly, so households want 75% of their wealth in bills instead of 70% (eq. 10). Wealth is unchanged at 100: they want +5 of bills and −5 of cash.

2 · Someone has to supply those bills. The government issues nothing new (no deficit). If nobody sold, bill prices would be bid up and their yield would fall back below 3%. The target would be missed.

3 · The central bank sells from its portfolio. To hold \(r\) at 3%, it sells 5 of bills (eq. 14–15). Households pay with cash, which returns to the central bank and disappears: \(H\) falls from 30 to 25.

Key idea

Causality runs from the interest rate to money, not the other way round

The textbook story has the central bank cutting the money supply to push up \(r\). Here it sets \(r\), and the quantity of money follows from households’ portfolio choice. GDP does not move: in this simple model, spending does not depend on \(r\).

D · Method

Two shocks you will not have to run

1

Liquidity preference \(\lambda_0\): 0.40 → 0.45

A purely financial shock: households want more cash.

2

Wealth effect \(\alpha_2\): 0.16 → 0.20

A real shock: households spend more out of their wealth.

For each one we follow the same order as in your assignment: GDP, private wealth, the international equilibrium, government finance, the central bank. The model’s main results come out along the way.

D · Shock 1 · Liquidity preference

Households want more cash: the central bank supplies it

\(\lambda_0\) rises from 0.40 to 0.45: at \(r = 2\%\), households now want 65% of their wealth in bills instead of 70% (eq. 10).

  • Portfolio: wealth stays at 100. Households sell 5 of bills and want 5 more cash (eq. 11).
  • Central bank: to stop the bill yield from rising above 2%, it buys those 5 bills and pays with new cash. \(B_{cb}\) goes from 20 to 25, \(H\) from 30 to 35. Reserves do not move.
  • Real side: nothing changes. GDP, consumption, wealth, the trade balance and the budget stay at their baseline values.
Result

The money supply is demand-determined

Money grew by 5 without any decision to “print money” and without any real effect. \(H_s = H_d\) holds automatically: this is the hidden equation at work.

Permanent shock from period 5. Same equations and baseline as OPEN_SFC.

Mirror image of the r shock seen in Part C: there the demand for cash fell, here it rises. In both cases the central bank accommodates.
D · Shock 2 · Wealth effect · GDP and wealth

Spending more out of wealth: a boom that fades

\(\alpha_2\) rises from 0.16 to 0.20 (eq. 5).

  • Impact (period 5): with \(V_{-1} = 100\), consumption out of wealth rises by \(0.04 \times 100 = 4\). Through the multiplier, \(Y\) rises by \(1.64 \times 4 = 6.6\).
  • Leakages: part of the extra income goes to taxes (\(\theta \Delta Y = 1.3\)) and imports (\(\mu \Delta Y = 1.6\)).
  • Wealth falls: households consume more than their disposable income, so \(\Delta V = Y - T - C = -2.95\) (eq. 2).
  • Feedback: lower wealth means less consumption out of wealth next period. \(C\) and \(Y\) slide back.
  • New steady state: \(Y^* = (G+X)/(\theta+\mu) = 100\) does not contain \(\alpha_2\), so GDP returns to 100. Wealth settles at \(V^* = (1-\alpha_1)(1-\theta)Y^*/\alpha_2 = 80\).

Permanent shock from period 5. Same equations and baseline as OPEN_SFC.

Behaviour towards wealth changes the level of stocks for good, but flows only for a while. Target wealth-to-disposable-income ratio alpha3 goes from 1.25 to 1.
D · Shock 2 · Wealth effect · international equilibrium

Where does the household dissaving go?

Add up the flow-of-funds rows of Table 1: \(\Delta V - \Delta B - \Delta R = 0\), so every period

\[\Delta V = (G - T) + (X - IM)\]
  • Period 5: \(-2.95 = -1.31 + (-1.64)\). Higher income brings in more taxes: the budget moves into surplus and the government retires bills. Higher imports push trade into deficit.
  • Transition: as \(Y\) returns to 100, all three balances go back to zero.
  • Stocks: the 20 of wealth lost are split between public debt (−8.9) and foreign reserves (−11.1). Still \(V = B + R\): \(80 = 81.1 - 1.1\).

Permanent shock from period 5. Same equations and baseline as OPEN_SFC.

This is Class 4’s sectoral balances identity, now with a mechanism behind each term. Point out that reserves end slightly negative: next slides.
D · Shock 2 · Wealth effect · central bank

Reserves fall, cash falls, yet the central bank holds more bills

The central bank’s balance sheet: \(H = R + B_{cb}\) (eq. 17).

  • Reserves fall (eq. 16): every period of trade deficit, importers buy foreign currency from the central bank and pay with domestic cash. Cumulated loss: 10 → −1.1.
  • Cash falls, but less: households keep 30% of a shrinking wealth in cash, \(H\) goes from 30 to 24 (eqs. 10–11).
  • So bills must rise. Losing 11.1 of reserves would drain 11.1 of cash, but households only want to give up 6. To keep \(r\) on target, the central bank buys the difference: \(B_{cb}\) goes from 20 to 25.1.
  • Same story from the bill market (eq. 14): households sell 14 of bills (70% of 20), the government retires only 8.9. The central bank absorbs the other 5.1.

Permanent shock from period 5. Same equations and baseline as OPEN_SFC.

Opposite movements of bills and cash are what endogenous sterilisation looks like in a deficit country. The paper shows the surplus case (Table 2).

Source: Godley & Lavoie (2006), Table 2, p. 251.

We just saw it for a deficit: reserves fell, and the central bank bought bills. The paper shows the surplus case. Reserves rise by 3; the textbook expects the money supply to rise by 3.

It rises by only 0.4, because households only want 0.4 more cash. The central bank keeps \(r\) on target by selling 2.6 of T-bills.

Nobody decides to sterilise: it follows from targeting an interest rate.

Key idea

The “rules of the game” do not apply

There is no automatic link from reserves to money, to interest rates, to the trade balance. Nothing in the model pushes exports back towards imports.

D · Result 2

Twin deficits in the quasi steady state

We saw it with shock 2: every period,

\[\Delta V = (G - T) + (X - IM)\]

When wealth has stopped growing (\(\Delta V = 0\)):

\[IM - X = G - T\]

A trade deficit goes with an equal budget deficit. This is a quasi steady state: flows are constant, but reserves and public debt keep changing every period.

Why “twin” only at the end

During the transition, households accumulate or run down wealth, so the private balance absorbs part of the gap. The identity holds every period; the twin-deficit result needs \(\Delta V = 0\) (footnote 15).

With the \(\alpha_2\) shock, all balances return to zero because \(Y^*\) does not change. Shocks that move \(Y^*\) away from \(X/\mu\) leave twin deficits or twin surpluses: look for them in your simulations.

D · Result 3

The external constraint and the super steady state

A deficit country loses reserves every period. When they run out, it must restrict demand until \(X = IM\). Then all stocks and all flows are constant:

\[Y^{**} = \frac{X}{\mu} = \frac{G}{\theta}\]
  • Output is set by exports relative to the import propensity: Harrod’s foreign-trade multiplier, whose growth version is Thirlwall’s law (Class 4, Part D).
  • Fiscal policy loses its autonomy: \(G/\theta\) has to adjust to \(X/\mu\).
Key idea

An asymmetric world

Deficit countries are forced into austerity when reserves run out. Surplus countries face no such limit: a central bank can accumulate foreign T-bills and sterilise indefinitely, as China did under its dollar peg. Keynes made the same point at Bretton Woods.

Check

Exports are 25 and the propensity to import is 0.25. What is the highest GDP this country can sustain without running down its reserves?

D · Reading the simulations

What does a negative stock mean?

The model has no floor on any stock. A stock below zero is not a bug: it tells you which institution would have to change sides, and whether the situation can last.

Reserves below zero

Reserves are balances held with foreign central banks (p. 248). Below zero, the central bank owes foreign currency: it has borrowed from other central banks or the IMF. Without capital flows, this cannot last. The country must restrict demand until \(X = IM\) (p. 252), or the peg breaks.

Central bank bills below zero

Households want more bills than the government has issued, typically after reserves have piled up. The central bank must sell bills it does not have: in practice it issues its own bills, or the government holds deposits with it (fn. 27, p. 270). China’s central bank bills worked this way.

Public debt below zero

After a long run of budget surpluses, the government has repaid all its bills and becomes a net creditor. It now holds assets, for example deposits at the central bank or a sovereign wealth fund.

Key idea

The asymmetry again

Negative reserves signal a constraint that will bind. Negative central bank bills only signal a change in instruments: a surplus country can keep sterilising.

D · Wrapping up

Three textbook claims, revisited

The textbook view (Mundell–Fleming, “rules of the game”)What the SFC model finds
A balance-of-payments surplus raises reserves and therefore the money supply, unless the central bank deliberately “sterilises”.Sterilisation is automatic when the central bank targets an interest rate. Money is demand-determined (shock 1, Result 1).
Trade imbalances correct themselves, through money, interest rates and prices.Nothing forces exports back to imports. A trade deficit can last as long as reserves do (Result 2).
Deficit and surplus countries face symmetric pressures.Deficit countries must eventually adjust; surplus countries never have to (Result 3).
D · Going further optional

The same logic with two countries and a floating exchange rate

The second part of the paper (pp. 254–271) lets residents of two countries trade each other’s T-bills. The exchange rate clears the market for internationally held bills.

  • Fiscal expansion raises output permanently and depreciates the currency.
  • A higher interest rate appreciates the currency at once, slows the economy, then wears off: its effect is temporary.

The Mundell–Fleming result, reversed

Mundell–Fleming concludes that fiscal policy is ineffective under floating rates. The difference comes from assuming that the central bank fixes the money stock instead of the interest rate.
