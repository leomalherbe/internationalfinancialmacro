A · Reading the balance of payments

A record of flows between a country and the rest of the world

The balance of payments (BoP) records flows, not stocks, between a country and the rest of the world over a given period (a quarter, a year). It measures:

  • payments made by residents — individuals, firms, organisations, government agencies — to non-residents;
  • payments made by non-residents to residents.
+ Credit

a payment received from the rest of the world.

− Debit

a payment made to the rest of the world.

Key idea

Signs give a direction, not a judgement

“+” and “−” only tell us which way a payment flows. Inflows are not “good” and outflows are not “bad”: every economy, rich or poor, records both debits and credits. The first step is to split the BoP into a current account and a financial account.

A · The current account

The current account: everything except trade in assets

1 · Goods & services

\(X - IM\)

Exports minus imports of goods, and of services such as tourism (a foreign tourist spending in Paris is an export).

2 · Secondary income

\(Tr_f\)

Net transfers between governments, individuals and organisations: official development aid, NGO humanitarian aid, migrants’ remittances.

3 · Primary income

\(r_f\)

Returns paid to and received from abroad: interest, dividends, rents, profits. Their net contribution can be positive or negative.

Current account balance \(= (X - IM) + Tr_f + r_f\)
A · The financial account

The financial account: international trade in assets

1 · Foreign direct investment

Acquiring a lasting stake in a foreign firm — as a rule, more than 10% of its shares. Building a factory abroad, or buying a foreign manufacturer outright.

2 · Portfolio investment

Shares and bonds bought without influence on management (less than 10% of a firm’s shares).

3 · Official reserve assets

Currencies and assets bought and sold by central banks, for instance to influence the exchange rate.

Financial account balance \(= S_f\)
\(S_f > 0\) · net borrower

Capital inflows exceed outflows: the rest of the world accumulates claims on the domestic economy (e.g. foreigners buy domestic corporate bonds).

\(S_f < 0\) · net lender

Capital outflows exceed inflows: residents accumulate claims on the rest of the world (e.g. they buy foreign bonds).

Sign convention used throughout the course: S_f is the financial account balance measured as net capital inflows (increase in liabilities to non-residents minus increase in claims on non-residents). Note that the IMF's BPM6 presents the financial account the other way round (net acquisition of assets minus liabilities); warn students when they download IMF data.
A · Double-entry bookkeeping

The two accounts always offset each other

\[ \underbrace{(X - IM) + Tr_f + r_f}_{\text{current account}} \;=\; -\,\underbrace{S_f}_{\text{financial account}} \]
Current account deficit

must be matched by a financial account surplus of the same size: the country accumulates financial liabilities — in 2024, the United States and France.

Current account surplus

must be matched by a financial account deficit: the country accumulates claims on the rest of the world, recycling its trade surplus — in 2024, China and Germany.

In the data, two more items close the identity: the capital account (small: debt forgiveness, trade in trademarks and other intangible assets) and net errors and omissions (a statistical residual).

A · Interpreting the balance of payments

Read the system, not the signs

Debits and credits are strictly equal: they record interdependent, balanced flows of goods, services and assets.

So we should not judge a single item by its sign. We need to understand how the economy fits into the world economy.

Example · the United States
  1. The trade deficit shows that the US has been the main market for world production for 40 years.
  2. It is financed by net capital inflows that reinforce Wall Street’s hegemony in global finance.
  3. These inflows reflect investors’ appetite for dollar-denominated assets.
  4. Inequality — two sides of the same coin: the trade deficit means production (and industrial jobs) moved abroad, holding down wages; the capital inflows push up US asset prices and financial profits, whose gains go mostly to the top.
  5. Development and stability: savings of developing countries (e.g. central bank reserves) flow to the US instead of funding their own investment, and an oversized Wall Street becomes a source of systemic risk.
  6. The hegemony of the dollar, on which these imbalances rest, is now widely debated.
A · Debate

The US trade deficit: a cost, or an exorbitant privilege?

Since his first term, President Donald Trump has taken a resolutely mercantilist stance (video). Three accounting facts to keep in mind:

1

A financial surplus is the counterpart of a current deficit

A country that imports more than it exports necessarily receives more foreign capital.

2

In real terms, exports are a cost and imports are income

Resources used to produce exports are not available for residents. For a firm, who buys its output does not matter in itself.

3

One country’s exports are another’s imports

Not all countries can be net exporters. Those in an unfavourable position race to austerity, feeding a general slump.

B · From flows to stocks

The external position: a stock at a point in time

BoP

Flows over a period (a quarter, a year).

EP

The net stock of foreign assets at a date (31 December).

A country’s external position (EP, or net international investment position) is the foreign assets held by its residents minus the domestic assets held by non-residents.

It reflects (i) past purchases of assets recorded in the financial account, and (ii) revaluations: changes in asset prices and in exchange rates.

+Net purchases of foreign assets by residents (financial account)
−Net purchases of domestic assets by non-residents (financial account)
±Price changes: shares and bonds held on both sides gain or lose market value
±Exchange-rate changes: a weaker dollar lowers the euro value of dollar assets held by euro area residents
±Other changes: write-offs (e.g. debt cancellations), reclassifications
=Change in the net external position

The last three lines are valuation effects: they move the stock without any transaction in the BoP.

B · Data

External positions of the United States and China, 2016–2024

The US net external position falls from −$8 trillion to −$26.5 trillion in eight years: the stock counterpart of persistent current account deficits — and of rising US asset prices held by foreigners.

China is a net creditor, at about +$3.3 trillion in 2024.

Valuation effects matter: in 2022, the US position improved although the current account deficit widened, as US equity prices fell.

Source: IMF, Balance of Payments and International Investment Position statistics. US$ trillions.
B · External debt dynamics

The law of motion of the external position

Let \(EP\) be the country’s net external debt (net liabilities to the rest of the world):

\[ EP_t = (1+r)\,EP_{t-1} - NX_t \]
\(r\)interest rate paid on the external debt\(NX\)net exports (trade balance)\(t\)time

Same structure as public debt in Class 3, with net exports in the role of the primary balance.

Divide by GDP and use \(Y_t = (1+g)\,Y_{t-1}\):

\[ \frac{EP_t}{Y_t} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\,\frac{EP_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}} - \frac{NX_t}{Y_t} \]

With \(ep = EP/Y\) and \(nx = NX/Y\):

\[ ep_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, ep_{t-1} - nx_t \]
B · Stabilising the external position

Which trade balance keeps \(ep\) constant?

Rearrange for net exports:

\[ nx_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, ep_{t-1} - ep_t \]

A stable position means \(ep_t = ep_{t-1}\):

\[ nx_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, ep_{t-1} - \frac{1+g}{1+g}\, ep_{t-1} = \frac{r-g}{1+g}\, ep_{t-1} \]

Growth rarely exceeds 5%, so \(1+g \approx 1\):

\[ nx^{*} \approx (r-g)\, ep \]
\(r<g\)

A trade deficit is compatible with a stable EP.

\(r=g\)

A balanced trade balance stabilises it.

\(r>g\)

A trade surplus is necessary.

B · Worked example

When investors change their mind

Calm markets

External debt \(ep = 35\%\) of GDP, growth \(g = 5\%\), interest rate \(r = 3\%\):

\(nx^* \approx (r-g)\,ep = (3\% - 5\%) \times 35\% = -0.7\%\) of GDP

The position stabilises with net exports above −0.7% of GDP: a small trade deficit is fine.

Risk premium of 7 pp

A change in sentiment: foreign investors suddenly demand \(r = 3\% + 7\% = 10\%\):

\(nx^* \approx (r-g)\,ep = (10\% - 5\%) \times 35\% = +1.75\%\) of GDP

The country must now run a trade surplus of 1.75% of GDP — a swing of almost 2.5 points.

The external position reflects, at the same time, international interest rates, economic growth and the current account.

C · Deriving the equation

Three sectors, one identity

Gross national income (GNI), from the uses side — with \(S_c\) the current account, so \(S_c = -S_f\):

\[ GNI = C + I + G + S_c = C + I + G - S_f \quad (1) \]

… and from the allocation side — consume, save or pay taxes:

\[ GNI = C + S + T \quad (2) \]

Setting (1) = (2):

\[ \underbrace{(S - I)}_{\text{private}} + \underbrace{(T - G)}_{\text{public}} + \underbrace{S_f}_{\text{external}} = 0 \]

The balances of the private sector, the public sector and the rest of the world always sum to zero.

For one sector to accumulate financial assets, another must go into debt by the same amount: “it takes two to tango” (Wray, 2018).

C · Reading the identity

Private balance + public balance + external balance = 0

Private sector

\(S - I\): income minus spending of households and firms. A surplus means net lending.

Public sector

\(T - G\): the budget balance. A deficit is negative.

External sector

\(S_f\): the financial account — the claims the rest of the world accumulates on the economy (capital inflows).

Because the financial account mirrors the current account (net of errors and omissions), a current account deficit shows up as a positive external balance.

The identity holds for the accounting of any currency: within a country, flows in a foreign currency also accumulate into stocks and obey their own balance equation.
C · Data

France’s sectoral balances, 1980–2025

Public deficits are mirrored mainly by private surpluses; the external balance stays within ±3% of GDP. Without a structural trade surplus, it is the public deficit that allows the French private sector to accumulate net financial wealth.

1997–2001: the private surplus exceeds the public deficit — the excess is lent abroad (external balance < 0: a current account surplus).

2009–10 and 2020: recessions. Public deficits reach 7–9% of GDP ↔ private surpluses as households and firms cut spending.

D · The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis

The terms of trade: a country’s international purchasing power

The terms of trade divide the average price of exports \(P_X\) by the average price of imports \(P_M\):

\[ ToT = \frac{P_X}{P_M} \times 100 \]

Below 100: a given volume of exports buys fewer imports. Above 100: it buys more.

To pay for imports without borrowing abroad: \(P_X Q_X = P_M Q_M\). If \(ToT = 50\), then \(P_X = 0.5\,P_M\) and \(Q_X = 2\,Q_M\) — the country must export twice as much.

D · Data

A century of falling commodity prices

Prebisch and Singer (1950): the prices of primary commodities tend to fall relative to manufactured goods.

The world agricultural price index loses about three quarters of its value between the 1910s and the early 2000s.

Countries exporting raw materials must export ever more volume to buy the same imports.

World agricultural price (Grilli–Yang) index, 1900–2015, price relative to 1977–1979.
D · Data

… and volatile ones: cocoa and coffee since 1990

Beyond the trend, commodity prices swing violently: booms and busts of 50% or more within a few years.

For a country whose exports rely on one or two crops, the current account — and the ability to service external debt — follows these cycles.

Link with Part B: falling export prices lower \(nx\) just when lenders raise \(r\).

D · Thirlwall’s law

Growth constrained by the balance of payments

\(x\)annual growth rate of exports\(m\)annual growth rate of imports\(y\)domestic GDP growth\(z\)foreign (world) GDP growth\(\varepsilon\)income elasticity of exports\(\Pi\)income elasticity of imports
\[ \begin{cases} x = \varepsilon z \\ m = \Pi y \end{cases} \]
Long run, \(x = m\):\[ y = \frac{x}{\Pi} = \frac{\varepsilon\, z}{\Pi} \]

Growth equals export growth divided by the income elasticity of imports.

D · Conclusion

Free trade does not benefit everyone equally

1

Growth in the periphery is capped by the balance of payments: import needs grow faster than export earnings.

2

Deteriorating terms of trade tighten the constraint further: more exports are needed for the same imports.

3

Free trade does not bring the same benefits to countries at different stages of development, and does not unconditionally promote development.
