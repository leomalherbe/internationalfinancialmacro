A · What MacSim 2 is

A macroeconomic simulator, not a forecasting tool

  • Ten advanced economies, each with the same New-Keynesian model: 50–60 equations, 10–15 of them estimated on quarterly data (error-correction form), the rest accounting identities.
  • Linked by trade (a bilateral trade matrix), by interest rates and by exchange rates; the rest of the world is passive.
  • Every result is a deviation from a reference path: what changes because of what you — and the others — did. Not a forecast.
  • Jean-Louis Brillet, Gilbert Cette & Ian Gambini (INSEE, Banque de France); MacSim 2, Economica, 2012.

Why a game?

Each team is a government. You choose policies, the model computes the consequences — for you and for everybody else — and one number, the objective function, says how well you did.

The point is not to win: it is to see the mechanisms of classes 1–5 act together, and to feel what interdependence does to a national policy.

Stress "deviation from reference": students will read tables of +0.11 % and −0.07 pt, not levels. The reference path is the same for everyone; the administrator can disturb it with shocks.

A · The objective

One number to maximise: the objective function

\( R_{i,t} \;=\; a_1\,\Delta \text{GDP}_{i,t} \;-\; a_2\,\Delta U_{i,t} \;-\; a_3\,\Delta \pi_{i,t} \;+\; a_4\,\Delta \text{PB}_{i,t} \;+\; a_5\,\Delta \text{TB}_{i,t} \)
+GDPvolume, gap in %
−Unemploymentrate, gap in points
−Inflationconsumer prices, gap in points
+Public balancegap in points of GDP
+Trade balancegap in points of GDP

Δ = deviation from the reference path, for that country and that year. The weights \(a_i\) are set in the software; the software computes \(R\) for every country every year, and the ranking follows it.

Reading: \(R = +0.10\) means “a tenth of a point of GDP better than doing nothing”, once unemployment, prices and the two balances are netted out.

The five components are the classic objectives of macro policy (the "magic square" plus the public balance). Insist on the signs: unemployment and inflation enter negatively.
A · The objective

Why the five objectives fight each other

A demand stimulus (spending ↑, taxes ↓)

demand→GDP→unemployment
…butimportspricespublic balance

GDP and jobs improve; the trade balance, the budget and inflation pay.

A supply-side move (labour costs ↓)

costs→prices→competitiveness→exports
…butpublic balanceandslow

Inflation and trade improve, GDP only gradually; the budget pays for the cut.

Key idea

Every lever moves several objectives, with opposite signs — that is why the game is a game

A good policy is a mix that targets each objective with the instrument that hurts the others least, and a timing that lets the slow effects arrive before the costs accumulate.

How a value is read

Each value is a relative change, in %, from the country’s own reference level, for that year. VAT +5 means the VAT rate rises by 5 % of its level (from 20 % to 21 %), not by 5 points.

0 = back to the reference. A measure kept for three years is entered three times.

Bounds in our game: ±10 % per lever; tariffs ±10 points.

VAT sits on the supply side but hits demand too: higher prices cut real wages and consumption.

A · The shocks

And the administrator holds the dice

Global shocks

  • World demand: the rest of the world buys more, or less, from everyone — each country hit according to its openness.
  • Exchange rate vs the rest of the world: competitiveness and import prices move for all.
  • Terms of trade and world tariffs: the price of what you import, the access to foreign markets.

Country-specific shocks

  • Total or active population, total factor productivity, average wages, the risk premium on sovereign debt.

How you will learn about them

Through the administrator’s communiqué on your page, at the start of each round — and in your results, where the report separates the effects of the shocks from the effects of your policy and of the other countries’ policies.

Shocks are what a government actually faces: you do not choose the storm, you choose the sails.

A · The rhythm

How the session unfolds

1

Warm-up

Every group governs France for three years. The other countries do nothing. A plan is submitted in one go, with a written strategy; the report comes back; you explain what happened.

2

The game, year by year

One country per group. The administrator announces the year’s shocks; you decide for the year; the simulation runs; everybody reads the results and the ranking; next year.

3

The council of ministers

At some point the council meets: pledges are public, decisions remain secret until the year is run. Then the gap between words and deeds is revealed.

One licence, one administrator: you submit on your page (one PIN per group), the administrator types the decisions into MacSim and feeds the report back to the page. Nothing of what other teams decide is visible until the round is closed.

A · The report

What comes back after each run

For your country and year, five deviations from the reference path and the value of \(R\). Each one is split in three:

  • effects of the shocks — the administrator’s doing
  • your own policy — what you would have obtained in a world where nobody else moved
  • foreign policies — what the other governments did to you, through trade, prices and interest rates

B · Architecture

Five blocks per country, and two optional loops

Solid arrows: always on. Dashed: the financial options chosen by the administrator. In our game: short-term rates follow a Taylor rule plus a risk premium on public deficits; exchange rates follow purchasing-power parity; the euro area shares one rate and one currency.

All ten countries have the same equations; only the estimated coefficients and the trade matrix differ.

After Brillet, Cette & Gambini (2012), “Real and financial economy interactions”.
B · Demand

Households consume income; firms invest when demand and profits justify it

Consumption

\( \Delta C = 0.24\,\Delta \text{Income} \;-\; 0.40\,\Delta U \;-\; 0.05\,\Delta \pi \)
  • a fraction of disposable income (net of taxes and contributions)…
  • …lower when unemployment rises (precautionary saving)…
  • …and when inflation rises (real balances).

Investment

\( K = 0.05\,K^{D} + 0.95\,K_{-1} + 0.10\,\text{Profit}, \quad K^{D} = \text{TFP}\cdot\text{GDP} - 0.65\,CK \)
  • the desired capital stock follows demand (accelerator) and falls with the cost of capital \(CK = P\,(r_{LT} - \delta + 0.02)\)
  • profits speed the adjustment; the long-term interest rate slows it.

Hence the first chain of the game: public demand or lower taxes → income → consumption → GDP → investment. And its leaks: part of the demand goes to imports, part is saved.

Coefficients: simplified reading of the MacSim 2 equations (course deck).
B · Supply and the labour market

Employment follows output and the cost of labour

\( L = \text{TFP}\cdot \text{GDP} - 0.35\,WC, \qquad U = \text{POP} - L \)
\( WC = (1+\tau)\,WB, \qquad WB = 0.56\,\pi \;-\; 2\,\Delta U \;+\; \text{residual} \)
  • Cobb-Douglas production; employment \(L\) rises with GDP and falls with the labour cost \(WC\) = wage bill × (1 + employer contribution rate \(\tau\)).
  • Wages index on inflation and fall when unemployment rises — a wage–price loop with a NAIRU for each country.
  • Unemployment is the gap between the active population and employment; public employment adds to \(L\) directly.

Which levers act here

  • Employer contributions (3): labour cost → employment, prices
  • Public employment (1): jobs now, a wage bill forever
  • Relative public wage (10): a cost with no output
  • TFP shock: more output per worker — and fewer workers for a given output
B · Prices

Inflation comes from costs and from tensions

\( \pi = \alpha\,\Delta \text{Costs} \;+\; 0.19\,\Delta UT \;+\; \text{residual} \)
  • Costs: wages, employer contributions, VAT, import prices — so labour-cost cuts and tariffs both show up here, with opposite signs.
  • Capacity utilisation \(UT\): when demand runs ahead of capacity, firms raise prices — the first restoring force against a demand stimulus.
  • A price-wage loop (WS–PS logic): prices → wages → costs → prices, damped by unemployment.

B · Trade

Your imports are their exports

\( \Delta M_{i,j} = 1.0\,\Delta \text{GDP}_i \;-\; 0.49\,\frac{MP_j}{DP_i} \;+\; 0.047\,UT_i, \qquad X_{j,i} = M_{i,j} \)
\( \Delta XP_{i,j} = 0.27\,\Delta\pi_i + 0.14\,\Delta\lambda_{i,j} + \text{tariffs}_{i,j} \)
  • Imports of country \(i\) rise one-for-one with its GDP, fall with the price competitiveness of domestic firms (import price over domestic price), rise when domestic capacity is saturated.
  • The share of each supplier \(j\) in \(i\)’s imports depends on \(j\)’s export prices and on how saturated \(j\) is.
  • Export prices rise with domestic inflation and with an appreciation of the currency; a tariff raises them for the buyer.

Three consequences for players

  • A stimulus at home is a gift to your suppliers: trade deficit for you, extra exports for them.
  • Inflation at home prices your exporters out — unless the exchange rate compensates.
  • Your tariff on Germany raises your import prices (inflation ↑) and Germany’s loss is other suppliers’ gain.
B · Money and exchange rates

The central bank reacts; the exchange rate follows prices

Short-term rate: a Taylor rule plus a risk premium

\( r_{ST} = \pi + 0.5\,(\pi - 2\%) + 0.5\,(UT - UT_{LT}) + \text{Premium} \)
\( \text{Premium} = a\,(\text{PB} - \text{PB}_{LT}) \qquad r_{LT} = 0.5\,r_{ST} + 0.5\,r_{ST,-1} + c \)

Inflation above 2 % or demand above capacity → rates up; a public deficit above its long-run value → a premium on top. Long rates smooth short rates and drive investment.

Exchange rate: purchasing-power parity

\( \Delta \lambda_{i,j} = \pi_j - \pi_i \)

Your currency depreciates when your inflation exceeds your partner’s — restoring competitiveness, slowly. Inside the euro area there is nothing to restore: one rate, one currency, set on the average member.

Which is why an inflationary stimulus in a euro member is a transfer of competitiveness to the others.

Link with class 2 (Taylor rule, term structure) and class 4 (exchange-rate regimes). The risk premium links the budget to the cost of capital: the public balance is not only an objective, it is a transmission channel.
B · Restoring forces

Why policies fade: three tensions, three brakes

Tension indicatorWhere it bitesHorizon
Capacity utilisation (product market)prices ↑, exports ↓, imports ↑transitory
Unemployment vs NAIRU (labour market)wages, prices, external and internal demandmedium to long term
Public balance (in points of GDP)short and long interest rates → household and firm demandmedium term
Rule 1

Demand policies do not move the long-run equilibrium

They buy output now; prices, interest rates and imports take it back over the years.

Rule 2

Only supply policies move the NAIRU, productivity or participation

Slower to act, but their effects last — the “sustainable growth strategy” of the model’s authors.

Brillet, Cette & Gambini (2012), §V.2.
B · Two experiments

What one point of policy buys — in France, and in Germany next door

Demand shock: public demand +1 point of GDP. Year 1: GDP +0.56 %, trade balance −0.44 pt, public balance −0.48 pt. Year 8: GDP +0.41 % only, prices +0.40 %.

Supply shock: employer contributions −1 point. Year 1: GDP +0.22 %, prices −0.56 %. Year 8: GDP +0.90 %, trade balance +0.25 pt.

Germany, which did nothing, gains +0.10 % of GDP and a better trade balance from the French stimulus — the “foreign policies” line of its report.

Brillet, Cette & Gambini (2012), §VI.2, simulations on France; deviations from the reference account.

C · The assignment

Govern France for three years — alone

The setting

Every group governs France; the nine other economies keep their policies unchanged. The administrator has applied the same initial shock to everyone — read the communiqué on your page.

The instruments

A policy for years 1, 2 and 3 among six levers: public employment, public demand, social benefits, income tax, employer contributions, VAT. At most four, each within ±10 % of its reference level.

Before the run: your strategy

150 characters at least: which objective each lever targets, through which mechanism, what you expect for the five components of \(R\). One submission per group, revisable until the round closes.

After the run: your analysis

Your page shows the five deviations, \(R\) and the breakdown. Explain the gaps with your forecast: which restoring force, which leak, which timing you had missed.

Objective

Maximise \(R\) over three years — and predict the sign and the size of each result before you see it

Being right for the right reasons is worth more than a good \(R\). Debrief: all plans and all results side by side.

C · Before you click

Design a policy like an economist, not like a gambler

1

Which objective?

Pick the components of \(R\) you go for, and those you accept to lose. You cannot win all five alone.

2

Which channel?

For each instrument, trace the chain to the objective — and the side chains to the other four (prices, imports, budget, interest rates).

3

Which timing?

Demand acts now and fades; supply acts late and lasts. A three-year profile is a choice, not three copies of year 1.

Orders of magnitude

From the test run: public employment +1 % → GDP +0.11 % in year 1, unemployment −0.07 pt, prices +0.10 pt, both balances −0.04 pt

A +10 % shock on one lever is about ten times that. Write your expected signs and sizes in the strategy box: the debrief compares them with the report.

D · After the warm-up

One country each, year by year, with everybody else moving

  • Each group receives a country: Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, United Kingdom, United States, Japan.
  • Each year: the administrator’s communiqué (the shocks), a timer, your decisions for the year with a short justification, the run, the results and the ranking on the board.
  • All eleven levers are open, within ±10 %; leaving or joining the euro and productivity shocks need the administrator’s consent.
  • Decisions stay secret while the round is open; once it is closed, everyone sees what everyone did.

The council of ministers

At some point the council is convened: each government may post a public pledge (“we stimulate by 0.5 if you do”). Decisions remain private until the year is run; afterwards the board shows who kept their word.

Coordination, free-riding, retaliation: the prisoner’s dilemma, lived before it is discussed.

D · To think about during the warm-up

What changes when the others play too?

  • Your stimulus leaks abroad through imports; theirs leaks to you. A joint stimulus has no trade-balance cost for anyone; a lonely stimulus pays for everybody’s recovery.
  • A cost cut at home is a competitiveness gain against partners who do nothing — and nothing at all if everybody does it.
  • Inside the euro area, the central bank reacts to the average: your inflation is partly their problem, their rigour is partly your interest rate.
  • Tariffs redirect trade more than they create it; expect an answer.

Read your warm-up report with this in mind: the line “foreign policies” is empty today. Next time it will be where the game is decided.
