A · The Treasury’s bank account

The Treasury banks at the central bank

The Treasury holds a reserve money account at the central bank — like commercial banks, and unlike households and firms.

  • United States: the Treasury General Account (TGA) at the Fed.
  • France: the Compte unique du Trésor (CUT) at the Banque de France.
Debited

when the Treasury spends: wages, pensions, purchases, interest paid.

Credited

when it receives money: taxes, and the money raised by selling bonds to investors.

Key idea 1

Money that reaches the Treasury’s account leaves the money supply

The Treasury’s balance is central bank money, like bank reserves: it is not part of M1 or M3. When you pay your taxes, your bank deposit disappears; when the Treasury spends, a new deposit appears.

Key idea 2

The Treasury’s account weighs on bank liquidity

Every euro credited to the Treasury is a euro of reserves taken from banks — and every euro it spends gives them back. The ECB forecasts government deposits every day to steer liquidity (an “autonomous factor”).

A · Data

Central government deposits with the Eurosystem, 1999–2025

For two decades, euro area governments kept €50–150 bn at the Eurosystem.

2020: the pandemic. Treasuries borrow massively and park the cash: deposits jump to about €750 bn.

From late 2022 they fall back to around €100 bn as balances are spent or moved to the market, when the Eurosystem changes how it remunerates government deposits.

A · A simple circuit view

Spending injects money, taxes withdraw it

A budget deficit (spending > taxes) is a net injection of deposits and net wealth into the private sector.

A budget surplus is a net withdrawal: the private sector’s financial wealth shrinks.

Government deficit \(=\) private sector surplus (in a closed economy)

One sector’s deficit is another sector’s surplus: we will return to this accounting identity when we open the economy.

B · The central bank’s balance sheet

Reserves and the Treasury’s account are two liabilities of the same institution

🏛️ Central bank
AssetsLiabilities & net worth
A1 Treasury bonds
A2 Other assets
L1 Banks’ reserve accounts
L2 Treasury’s account
L3 Other liabilities & net worth

L1 is approximately the monetary base (M0, net of cash).

\[ L_1 = A_1 + A_2 - L_2 - L_3 \]

Fiscal policy moves the Treasury’s account:

\[ \Delta L_2 = T + \Delta B - G \]

\(T\) taxes · \(\Delta B\) new bonds sold to the private sector · \(G\) spending. With assets unchanged, every euro that enters \(L_2\) leaves \(L_1\): \(\Delta L_1 = -\Delta L_2\).

B · Treasury operations and bank reserves

Every Treasury payment moves bank reserves

Spending

\(L_2\) ↓, \(L_1\) ↑: banks receive reserves, and the public receives new deposits.

Taxing

\(L_2\) ↑, \(L_1\) ↓: banks lose reserves, and the public’s deposits are destroyed.

Deficit (not bond-financed)

Spending > taxes: a net increase in \(L_1\) and in private deposits.

Surplus

Taxes > spending: a net decrease in \(L_1\) and in private deposits.

Recall Class 2: the interbank rate depends on reserve supply minus demand. So the Treasury’s daily flows push €STR away from target.

C · The question

A deficit must be financed — but by whom?

Part A: spending debits the Treasury’s account, taxes credit it. With a deficit (\(G > T\)), the account would run dry — so the gap must be filled by someone. That is the government’s budget constraint:

\[ \underbrace{G - T}_{\text{deficit}} \;=\; \underbrace{\Delta B_{CB}}_{\text{central bank}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta B_{P}}_{\text{private investors}} \]

\(\Delta B_{CB}\): new government debt taken up by the central bank (overdraft or bond purchases). \(\Delta B_{P}\): new bonds sold to banks and investors.

C · #1 Direct monetary financing

The central bank provides reserves to the Treasury on demand

Direct monetary financing occurs when the central bank provides reserves on demand to fund public expenditure.

Two channels:

  • an overdraft facility on the Treasury’s account;
  • purchases of government bonds by the central bank — on the primary or the secondary market.
The Bank of England, 1866–1968

“From (at least) 1866 to 1968, the UK Parliament annually voted to authorize the BoE to provide the total amount of public expenditure approved through the national budget process: Parliament would vote on estimates of national expenditure and then authorize the BoE to purchase the total amount of debt securities issued by the UK’s Treasury to fund that expenditure.”

— Bateman and van ’t Klooster (2024)

C · Example

The “arsenal of democracy”: financing World War II

US war bond posters and certificate, 1940s.

The US Treasury issued as many bonds as needed to pay for the war effort. The Fed supplied banks with the reserves to buy them, and bought large amounts itself (Federal Reserve History).

The Fed pegged rates: 0.375% on 3-month bills and about 2.5% on long-term bonds. Deficits were financed at minimal cost.

Military spending rose from a few hundred million dollars a year before the war to $85 bn in 1943 and $91 bn in 1944 — about 40% of US GDP.

C · #1 Direct monetary financing

A monetary “taboo”

As of 2026, direct monetary financing is officially forbidden in many countries — directly (constitutions of developing countries, European Treaties) or indirectly (IMF conditionality, World Bank and OECD advice).

For Bateman and van ’t Klooster (2024), it has become a taboo. According to the orthodoxy:

Mature, stable and successful economies resolutely abstain from monetary finance.

Irresponsible politicians and immature nations allow their central banks to monetise public spending.

Yet the 2008 crisis and the 2020 pandemic showed that governments do not hesitate to use monetary financing whenever necessary.

Source: Bateman and van ’t Klooster (2024).

C · #2 Sovereign bond issues

In the euro area, the Treasury must borrow before it spends

1992 · Maastricht Treaty

Does not stop the central bank from acting as a lender of last resort to governments via the secondary market, but prohibits direct transactions between central banks and national treasuries.

Article 123 TFEU

The Treaty on the Functioning of the EU (as amended at Lisbon) prohibits the central bank from financing governments or public bodies — no overdrafts, no primary-market purchases.

Consequence: euro area Treasuries have no overdraft facility at their national central bank. Before spending, they must raise taxes or issue bonds.

C · #2 Sovereign bond issues · Example

Who buys government bonds on the primary market?

🇩🇪 Germany

The Ministry of Finance (Finanzministerium) asks the German Finance Agency (Finanzagentur) to issue new securities. They are sold to an officially approved group of bidders, the Bietergruppe Bundesemissionen — a few dozen banks from the EU, the US and Japan.

🇫🇷 France

Same logic: the Agence France Trésor auctions OATs to its primary dealers, the spécialistes en valeurs du Trésor (SVT).

Inside a primary dealer

Most primary dealers are investment banks, or the investment banking arms of large banking groups.

Money market / repo desk→Sovereign securities desk

The repo desk raises funds on the money market; the sovereign desk uses them to buy public debt at auction. Entities without a reserve account settle through a commercial bank.

C · Can the Treasury go bankrupt?

Two ways to go bankrupt

Insolvency

Liabilities exceed assets.

Illiquidity

The institution cannot pay its bills on time, even though its assets exceed its liabilities.

Thought experiment: a French government bond (OAT) of €50 m matures, but tax revenues are insufficient. The government must urgently find €50 m to pay its bondholders.

As long as the OAT is denominated in domestic currency (the euro), this is not a problem: the Treasury rolls over the debt — it issues a fresh OAT to repay the maturing one, which is destroyed.

C · Can the Treasury go bankrupt?

No liquidity crisis — but the debt grows with the interest rate

The old OAT is repaid, but the debt has grown from 50 to 55 at a 10% rate. Good coordination with the central bank keeps the bond market from ballooning. In practice:

1

Most monetary systems let the central bank buy government bonds, which makes insolvency impossible — as long as interest is due in the domestic currency.

2

The central bank can lower its target rate to ease the pressure: short-term T-bill rates track the policy rate almost perfectly.

3

The Treasury can manage the maturity structure of its debt: issuing only short-term bills aligns its borrowing cost with the policy rate.

C · Data

Euro area spreads: the price of not issuing your own currency

Period

A spread = a country’s 10-year yield minus Germany’s: the extra price markets charge to lend to it.

1999–2007: spreads close to zero. Markets treat all euro debts as almost equivalent.

2010–12: spreads explode — above 12 pp for Portugal, above 5 pp for Italy (Greece, not shown, went much higher).

July 2012: “whatever it takes”. Spreads fall before the ECB buys a single bond: the promise is enough.

Since then, the ECB contains spreads with new instruments (PEPP 2020, TPI 2022). Debt sustainability depends on the central bank’s willingness to act.

Source: OECD via FRED & Banque de France — monthly 10-year yields; spread = yield − German yield (pp).
PEPP = Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (March 2020). TPI = Transmission Protection Instrument (July 2022), never activated so far. Note France’s spread rising to Italy’s level in 2024–25 (political and fiscal uncertainty).

D · Debt dynamics

The law of motion of public debt

\[ D_t = (1+r)\,D_{t-1} - P_t \]

\(D\) · public debt

The volume (stock) of public debt.

\(r\) · interest rate

The average (effective) interest rate on the debt.

\(P\) · primary balance

Taxes minus public spending, excluding interest. \(P>0\): surplus.

\(t\) · time

Each year, the debt grows with interest and shrinks with primary surpluses.

D · Debt dynamics

From levels to ratios

Divide by GDP \(Y_t\):

\[ \frac{D_t}{Y_t} = (1+r)\frac{D_{t-1}}{Y_t} - \frac{P_t}{Y_t} \]

GDP grows at the (nominal) rate \(g\):

\[ Y_t = (1+g)\,Y_{t-1} \]

Substitute \(Y_t\) in the first equation:

\[ \frac{D_t}{Y_t} = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\,\frac{D_{t-1}}{Y_{t-1}} - \frac{P_t}{Y_t} \]

With \(d = D/Y\) and \(p = P/Y\):

\[ d_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, d_{t-1} - p_t \]
D · Stabilising the debt ratio

Which primary balance keeps \(d\) constant?

Rearrange for the primary balance:

\[ p_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, d_{t-1} - d_t \]

A stable ratio means \(d_t = d_{t-1}\):

\[ p_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g}\, d_{t-1} - \frac{1+g}{1+g}\, d_{t-1} = \frac{r-g}{1+g}\, d_{t-1} \]

Since \(g\) rarely exceeds 5%, \(1+g \approx 1\), and:

\[ p^{*} \approx (r-g)\, d \]

The primary balance needed to stabilise the debt ratio depends on the gap between the interest rate and the growth rate, times the existing debt.

D · Conclusion

The debt ratio stabilises if \(p = (r-g)\,d\)

\(r < g\)

A primary deficit (\(p<0\)) is compatible with a stable debt ratio. A balanced budget makes the ratio fall.

\(r = g\)

A balanced primary budget (\(p=0\)) stabilises the ratio; a surplus reduces it.

\(r > g\)

A primary surplus (\(p>0\)) is necessary to stabilise the ratio. Otherwise the debt snowballs.

Link with Part C: the central bank largely sets \(r\). Debt sustainability is a matter of monetary–fiscal coordination, not of fiscal discipline alone.
