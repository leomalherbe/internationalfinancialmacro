A · Risk versus uncertainty

Three states of knowledge (Lavoie, 2016)

Certainty

Each choice invariably leads to a specific outcome, whose value is known.

Risk

Each choice leads to a set of outcomes with known values, each with a known probability.

Uncertainty

The value of outcomes, their probabilities, the possible outcomes — or even the set of possible choices — are unknown.

Financial markets are a world of uncertainty.

A · Risk versus uncertainty

Known unknowns… and unknown unknowns

“There are things we know we know. We also know that there are known unknowns, that is to say we know there are some things that we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns — the ones we don’t know we don’t know.”

— Donald Rumsfeld, 2003

Unknown unknowns are the outcomes of an action, or states of the world, we have no knowledge of — we do not even know they are possible.

With unknown unknowns we are in the realm of uncertainty: no probability can be computed.

A · Liquidity preference

Money is a special asset

Money (bank deposits, cash, T-bills) is not only a store of value: it commands purchasing power.

Liquidity = the speed at which an asset can be converted into money.

Liquidity preference is the desire to hold wealth in liquid form. A change in liquidity preference is a change in the composition of portfolios: agents shift their assets towards money (LP ↑) or towards less liquid assets (LP ↓).

To be held, less liquid assets must offer a return that compensates for the liquidity they lack. So the structure of interest rates reflects liquidity preference.

The liquidity ladder

Cash & deposits
T-bills
Long-term bonds
Equities
Real estate, loans
← more liquidless liquid →

The further right you go, the more you must be paid to give up liquidity: a higher expected return, i.e. a liquidity premium.

A · Liquidity preference

Why hold money? Keynes’ four motives

1 · Transaction motive

Households hold money to bridge the gap between the moment income is received and the moment it is spent.

2 · Finance motive

Firms need money to finance planned production and investment before sale proceeds come in (Keynes, 1937).

3 · Precautionary motive

Money provides for unexpected expenditures and lets agents seize unforeseen opportunities; its value is fixed in money terms.

4 · Speculative motive

Money as a category of financial investment, held in place of bonds when their prices are expected to fall.

Liquidity preference changes over time and across categories of agents.

A · Liquidity preference

When expectations falter, LP rises — on both sides of the economy

Real side
Expectations→LP→Investment & hiring

Businesses cut investment and employment plans.
→ transaction, finance and precautionary motives.

Financial side
Expectations→LP→Asset prices→Long rates

Banks and investors move to more liquid assets: stock and bond prices fall, long-term rates rise.
→ speculative motive.

B · The fundamental equality

Market clearing equalises risk-adjusted returns

If risk-adjusted returns differed, some assets would remain unsold. So in equilibrium:

\[ i_{CB} = i_{ST} - \sigma_{ST} = i_{LT} - \sigma_{LT} \]

\(i_{CB}\) target overnight rate of the central bank · \(i_{ST}, i_{LT}\) short- and long-term rates · \(\sigma\) the risk premium (discount) on each asset

  1. The central bank sets \(i_{CB}\): the base rate of the system.
  2. Other rates adjust to it, through their independent discounts \(\sigma\).
  3. A rise in liquidity preference raises the \(\sigma\)’s.
  4. Rates and asset prices = interplay of central bank policy and changes in LP.

\(i = i_{CB} + \sigma\): build the rate structure

B · In their words

The central bank sets the short end; LP sets the spread

“The monetary authorities set the rate at the short-term end of the spectrum, liquidity preference (along with other considerations) determines the mark-up to long term rates.”

— Dow and Dow (1989)

“Liquidity preference determines not the long rate of interest but the spread between the short and the long rates.”

— Wells (1983)

Caveat: if inflation expectations rise, a cut in the base rate may raise liquidity preference more than proportionally (\(\Delta i_{CB} + \Delta\sigma > 0\)): long rates increase.

B · Example

The Fed during the Covid crisis: 30-year rate = policy rate + premium

March 2020: the Fed cuts its rate from 1.58% to 0.05% in two steps.

But the risk premium on the 30-year bond jumps (0.07 → 1.4 pp): liquidity preference soars.

Result: from end-February to October the 30-year rate stays in a 1.2–1.7% band while the policy rate falls by 1.5 pp. The central bank controls the short end, not the long end.

Data: Fed funds rate and 30-year Treasury yield, Jan–Oct 2020. Risk premium = 30Y yield − Fed funds. Hover for values.

C · Objectives

The objectives of monetary policy

Core mission: maintain the integrity of the payment system. Other objectives depend on the public mandate: price stability, full employment, exchange rate management, sustainable finance…

In the euro area, the ECB implements its policy by setting three rates:

Ceiling

Marginal lending facility

A bank that needs reserves urgently (to settle interbank balances or pay out cash) borrows them overnight against collateral.

Main rate

Main refinancing operations (MRO)

The rate at which the ECB lends reserves against collateral — maturities of days, weeks, months or even years.

Floor

Deposit facility

Open to banks that want to park their excess reserves at the ECB.

C · Settlement

Banks are structurally indebted to the central bank

1 · Settlement. During the day, customers of banks A and B make many payments to each other. At the end of the day only the net amount is settled: here bank A pays 100 in reserves to bank B, by a transfer between their accounts at the central bank.

2 · A hierarchical system. Reserves (central bank money) can only be issued by the central bank: it holds the monopoly. Bank deposits are promises to pay in central bank money, so the central bank sits at the top of the banking hierarchy.

3 · Its role. Make sure bank A can access the reserves it needs, by issuing them through reserve loans — at a price it sets.

C · Where do reserves come from?

Bank A owes $100 of reserves it does not have

It gets them from the least costly source:

Sell assets

e.g. sell T-bills to other banks in exchange for reserves.

Interbank loan

Borrow reserves from other banks, domestic or foreign.

Central bank

Borrow from the central bank, the monopoly supplier of reserves (“high-powered money”).

The initial quantity of reserves in the system in no way constrains bank lending or deposit creation. What matters is the price of reserves: bank A profits as long as its loan rate exceeds the rate paid to the central bank — so the central bank rate drives lending and, ultimately, spending.

C · Counterparts of money

All money is created by a financing operation

Every deposit is created when the banking system acquires a claim: a loan, a bond, a foreign asset. In the consolidated balance sheet of the banking system, money (M3) sits on the liability side; its counterparts are the claims on the asset side.

M3 =  Credit to the private sector (firms, households)
       + Credit to general government (public sector)
       + Net external assets (rest of the world)
       − Longer-term financial liabilities
       ± Other counterparts

Longer-term liabilities (deposits > 2 years, bonds > 2 years, capital) are not money: when they grow, M3 shrinks.

C · Reserve issuance

The main refinancing instrument: repos

The central bank supplies reserves through repurchase agreements (repos):

1 · Collateral

The bank supplies eligible collateral for the loan’s maturity. The ECB sets the minimum rating (typically BBB by Fitch/S&P, Baa3 by Moody’s) and updates the list of eligible assets.

2 · Haircut

The central bank applies a haircut to the collateral and issues the loan. More assets with bigger haircuts, or fewer with smaller ones.

3 · Repurchase

At maturity the bank buys back its collateral at a higher price: loan + interest (e.g. 100 + 5% = 105), paid in reserves. The central bank returns the collateral.

A repo is thus a collateralised loan: interest = repurchase price − initial loan.

C · The money market

The interbank market

  • Banks with excess reserves after settlement lend them to banks that are short.
  • Also called the money market: what is traded is central bank money.
  • Banks can always borrow from the central bank → its lending rate is the upper limit of the interbank rate.
  • Reference rate: €STR (euro short-term rate, formerly EONIA).
  • The interbank rate is the central bank’s policy target.
Period
Until 2008 the overnight rate sits in the middle of the corridor, close to the MRO rate. With abundant reserves (full allotment from 2008, asset purchases from 2015) it falls to the floor: the deposit facility rate becomes the rate that matters.

C · Since 2015

From corridor to floor: the ECB’s balance sheet balloons

  • From 2015 the ECB moves to a highly accommodating policy to avoid the collapse of the financial system.
  • Implemented through asset purchase programmes (APP) paid with reserves: about €2,500 bn of assets held as of December 2025.
  • Banks must park their excess reserves at the deposit facility → €STR sits just below the deposit facility rate.

Why a floor in turbulent times?

  • No need for precision: as long as it is large enough, any supply of reserves works.
  • The target can be raised or lowered and €STR follows one-for-one.

D · The demand for money

\(L_d = L(r_L,\, Y,\, \pi)\)

The demand for money (credit) \(L_d\) is a decreasing function of the interest rate. Its slope reflects the level of liquidity preference.

It also depends on:

  • Expected income \(Y\): higher expected GDP → more money demanded (income motives).
  • Inflation \(\pi\): agents target a real amount of goods and services → higher prices raise money demand.
D · The supply of money

Banks set a price, then lend what is demanded

The money supply results from the interaction between the demand for loans and banks’ lending criteria (creditworthiness).

“The banking system fixes a set of rates and then lends however much borrowers ask for, provided that they can offer satisfactory collaterals.”

— Le Bourva (1992)

“Changes in the stock of loans and money will be governed solely by the demand for loans and the credit-worthiness of would-be borrowers.”

— Godley and Cripps (1983)

D · The lending rate of banks

A mark-up over the base rate

\[ r_L = \big[\,1 + \sigma + m(L)\,\big]\; r_F, \qquad m'(L) > 0 \]

\(r_F\) · bank base rate

The rate at which banks borrow reserves on the interbank market (or from the central bank).

\(\sigma\) · risk premium

Reflects banks’ liquidity preference: their confidence about an uncertain future.

\(m\) · banking mark-up

Target profit as a spread over \(r_F\) (the lending-to-deposit rate spread).

\(m(L)\) rises with lending

As loan demand grows, banks raise mark-ups to hedge against a deterioration in borrower quality.

D · Beyond the credit market

Closing the model

Interest rates, money demand and bank lending are inter-related. To connect them to the wider monetary system, we add:

1

The supply of reserves through open market operations

2

The balance sheet of banks

3

Banks’ reserve holdings

4

The central bank base rate

These are described by a set of six equations.

D · The model

Money and interest rates in six equations

(1)\( r_L = [1+\sigma+m(L)]\,r_F \)Loan rate: a mark-up over the money-market rate \(r_F\) set by the central bank (the cost of reserves).
(2)\( L_d = L(r_L,\, Y,\, \pi) \)Loan demand: decreasing in \(r_L\), increasing in income \(Y\) and inflation \(\pi\).
(3)\( L_s = L_d \)The credit market clears: loan supply equals loan demand.
(4)\( L_s + R = M \)Banks’ simplified balance sheet: loans + reserves = deposits.
(5)\( R = kM \)Reserves are a share \(k\) of deposits, set by the liquidity preference of the public.
(6)\( H = R \)The supply of high-powered money equals bank reserves.
D · Model resolution

Solving for loans, money and reserves

\[ L = L\big([1+\sigma+m(L)]\,r_F;\; Y;\; \pi\big) \] \[ M = \frac{L}{1-k} \] \[ H = kM = \frac{k\,L}{1-k} \]

Causality runs from right to left: from the policy rate to loans, from loans to deposits, and from deposits to reserves.

  1. Start from the target rate \(r_F\).
  2. Eq. (1) gives the loan rate \(r_L\).
  3. Given loan demand (2), we get the amount of loans \(L\).
  4. Loans determine deposits \(M\), depending on \(k\) (the slope of the BD line).
  5. Eq. (6): reserves \(H = R\) are supplied on demand by the central bank.

D · Reading the examples

Two shocks, one logic: the central bank accommodates

Example #1 · Policy rate cut
  • The loan supply curve shifts down (constant mark-ups and risk premia).
  • With unchanged money demand, loans increase.
  • This puts pressure on reserves.
  • To keep €STR from rising, the central bank buys Treasury bonds: \(H\) rises.
Example #2 · Expected GDP falls
  • Money demand shifts to the left.
  • With constant mark-ups and base rate, loans decrease.
  • Excess reserves appear.
  • To avoid a fall in €STR, the central bank sells bonds, debiting banks’ reserve accounts: \(H\) falls.
